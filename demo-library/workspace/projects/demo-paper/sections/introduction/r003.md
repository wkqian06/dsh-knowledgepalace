# Introduction

Drought is classified by the system it reaches — meteorological, agricultural, hydrological — but its damage is transmitted between them, from rainfall deficit to soil moisture, to vegetation, and on to yield and carbon exchange [1]. What the transmission carries is not simply the rainfall deficit: during the 2018 European drought, the response of five dominant land-cover classes varied significantly with the climatic water balance, which combines rainfall with the demand of the atmosphere [2]. The standardized precipitation evapotranspiration index was introduced to put that demand term into the deficit itself [3]. The quantity that governs impact is therefore the water state that soil and vegetation experience, not the rainfall anomaly alone.

Monitoring has been organized around the deficit. Global systems assemble near-real-time indicators into continuous maps, and some pair them with a seasonal probabilistic prediction module [4,5]. Local accuracy still rests on in-situ indices, which measure a single point and cannot represent how a drought develops across a region [6], while satellite records are limited by their length and by spatial, temporal and spectral resolution [7]. Monitoring is thus most complete where the deficit is measured and least complete where the impact is decided.

Forecasting has taken a parallel path. Its methods have broadened from statistical, dynamical and hybrid systems to machine learning and deep learning [8,9]. Studies of soil-moisture predictability now extend from seasonal to decadal horizons [10]. Two limits then appear. Human water use weakens the link: in the Yellow River basin, where water is abstracted and stored, the correlation between hydrological and meteorological drought is reduced [11]. Impact forecasts, which predict damage rather than deficit, depend greatly on the availability of impact records [12]. A forecast of the deficit is therefore not a forecast of the damage.

Damage depends on when and where the deficit lands, not only on how large it is. Crop yield loss is tied to the growth stages at which evaporative stress peaks [13]. Leaf-area sensitivity to soil moisture has increased across many semi-arid and arid regions [14], and the deficit at which vegetation responds varies from region to region [15]. In European forests, the relationship between water availability and mortality is threshold-like [16]. Global aggregates conceal this structure: no general drying trend was found across forests, but spatial variability was large [17]. Impact has no single global trajectory; it has thresholds that must be located region by region.

Onset speed adds a second dimension to the same problem. In the two decades since flash drought entered the literature, research on it has grown rapidly [18]. Agreement on what the term denotes, however, is incomplete: one line of work defines flash drought by its rate of intensification, another implicitly by its duration [19]. Frequency, predictability and trend estimates inherit that choice [20]. Whether flash drought has become the 'new normal' remains unresolved, since slow droughts may be increasing as well [21].

What remains unresolved is not detection but anticipation. Connecting the water states that forecasts can deliver [4,5,10] to the thresholds at which crops, vegetation and forests respond [13–16] would let a deficit be read as an expected loss, at the onset speeds for which a forecast still arrives in time [19,20]. That connection is the one the present literature does not yet make, and the question this article takes up.
